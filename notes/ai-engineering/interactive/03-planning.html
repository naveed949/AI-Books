Module 3

Planning AI Applications

"It's easy to build a cool demo with foundation models. It's hard to create a profitable product." Before building: why build it, what roles do AI and humans play, what's your moat, what does success look like — and can you survive the last mile?

3.1Why build this? The risk ladder

Building an AI app is a response to risks and opportunities. Click a rung — ordered from highest stakes to lowest.

"If you don't do this, competitors with AI can make you obsolete." Highest priority. Common where the business is document processing or information aggregation (financial analysis, insurance, data processing) and in creative work (advertising, web design, image production). Gartner 2023: 7% of 2,500 executives cited business continuity as their AI motivation. If the threat is existential → build in-house, don't outsource to a competitor.

"You'll miss chances to boost profits and productivity." The most common motive. AI can cut acquisition costs (copy, product descriptions, visuals), lift retention (support, personalization), and help with lead gen, internal comms, market research, competitor tracking. Here, buy options often beat building — cheaper, faster, sometimes better.

"Unsure where AI fits — but don't want to lag." Don't chase every hype train, but waiting too long has been fatal — cue Kodak, Blockbuster, BlackBerry. Investing to understand a transformational technology is reasonable if affordable (often lives in R&D at big companies; small startups may not afford even one person "looking around").

3.4Defensibility — what's your moat?

The low entry barrier cuts both ways: easy for you = easy for competitors. If the underlying model expands, it can subsume your layer (the PDF-parser-on-ChatGPT cautionary tale). A VC partner's test: what stops Google or Microsoft from replicating you with three engineers in two weeks?

Technology

  • With foundation models, most companies' core tech is similar — weak moat by default.

Data

  • Big firms hold more data, but a startup that ships first and gathers usage data can make data its moat — even unusable-for-training data reveals user behavior and product gaps ("data flywheel").

Distribution

  • Getting the product in front of users — advantage likely belongs to big companies.
Hope for the small: Calendly could've been a Google Calendar feature, Mailchimp a Gmail feature, Photoroom a Google Photos feature. Many winners began as a feature the giants overlooked.

3.6Milestones and the last-mile challenge

Evaluate off-the-shelf models first — the stronger they are, the less work remains (and evaluation may change your goals, or kill the project). Then beware: a good demo doesn't promise a good product. UltraChat: "the journey from 0 to 60 is easy, whereas progressing from 60 to 100 becomes exceedingly challenging."

3.7Maintenance — riding the bullet train

Building on foundation models means committing to AI's pace of change. Not all change is equal:

Good changes — that still cause friction
Longer context, better outputs, cheaper/faster inference (the chapter's Figure 1-11 shows cost per MMLU-level performance collapsing 2022→2024). But the best option today can be the worst tomorrow: build in-house, then providers halve prices; bet on a vendor, and it fails to raise. Run continuous cost-benefit analysis.
Easier changes — model swaps
Providers converge on similar APIs, so swapping is getting easier — but every model has quirks, so prompts, workflows, and data need re-tuning. Painless only with versioning and evaluation infrastructure in place.
Harder changes — regulation
AI is a national-security matter: compute, talent, and data are heavily regulated. GDPR compliance cost businesses an estimated $9B; compute access can change overnight (US October 2023 Executive Order). "If your GPU vendor is suddenly banned from selling GPUs to your country, you're in trouble."
Potentially fatal changes — IP
IP rules for AI are unsettled. If your product sits on a model trained on other people's data, will your product's IP stay yours? IP-heavy companies (e.g., game studios) hesitate for exactly this reason.
