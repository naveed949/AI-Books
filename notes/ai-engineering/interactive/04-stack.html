Module 4

The AI Engineering Stack

Ignore the shifting sand; learn the building blocks. Three layers, what changed from ML engineering, how models are adapted — and why evaluation became the big problem.

4.1Three layers — click each

Every AI application stack has three layers. You typically start at the top and move down as needed.

Reality check from GitHub (March 2024): Huyen counted 920 AI repos with ≥500 stars. After Stable Diffusion and ChatGPT, the biggest 2023 jumps were in applications and application development. Infrastructure grew least — because core infra needs (resource management, serving, monitoring) stay the same even as models and apps change.

4.2AI engineering vs. ML engineering

What stays the same

  • AI apps must solve business problems — map business ↔ ML metrics.
  • Systematic experimentation — just with prompts, models, retrieval, sampling variables instead of hyperparameters.
  • Making models faster and cheaper.
  • Feedback loops to improve with production data.

The three big shifts

  • 1 · Adaptation over training — you use a model someone else trained.
  • 2 · Bigger models — more compute, higher latency → inference optimization and GPU/cluster skills matter more ("we know 10 GPUs, not 1,000").
  • 3 · Open-ended outputs — flexible, but much harder to evaluate.
In short: AI engineering is less about model development, more about adapting and evaluating models.

4.3Model adaptation — does it touch the weights?

Every adaptation technique falls in one of two buckets:

Prompt-based — weights frozen

  • Adapt via instructions and context, not the model itself.
  • Easy to start, needs little data; lets you try many models fast (you might find one "unexpectedly good" for your task).
  • May fall short for complex tasks or strict performance requirements.

Finetuning — weights updated

  • Change the model itself by continuing to train it.
  • More complex, needs more data — but can significantly improve quality, latency, and cost.
  • Some things are impossible without it (e.g., a task the model never saw in training).

The training-terminology minefield

Pre-training
Training from scratch — weights randomly initialized; for LLMs, usually text completion. The most resource-hungry phase by far: 98% of InstructGPT's compute and data. Mistakes are expensive; expertise is rare and heavily sought after.
Finetuning vs. post-training
Both mean continuing to train a trained model — conceptually the same; needs far fewer resources. Usage differs by who does it: post-training when model developers do it (OpenAI teaching a model to follow instructions before release); finetuning when app developers adapt a released model. Pre- and post-training form a spectrum with similar processes and tooling.
Gotchas — what training is not
Quantization changes weight values (reduces precision) but is not training. And feeding your journal into ChatGPT to "train it to mimic you" is prompt engineering, not training or finetuning — teaching via context never touches the weights.

4.4Why evaluation got hard — the Gemini story

Close-ended ML tasks have ground truth; a chatbot's prompt has endless valid responses — no exhaustive answer key is possible. Worse, the adaptation technique changes the result. When Google launched Gemini (Dec 2023), it beat ChatGPT on MMLU — evaluated with CoT@32 (32 examples) while ChatGPT got 5. At an equal 5 examples, GPT-4 was ahead:

It's also why prompt engineering is real engineering — instructions, context, tools, and memory management for long tasks.

4.5AI interfaces & the reversed workflow

Pre-foundation-model AI hid inside big products (fraud detection in Stripe, recommenders in Netflix). Now anyone ships AI — standalone (ChatGPT, Perplexity) or embedded (Copilot in VSCode, Grammarly in the browser, Midjourney in Discord); via web/desktop/mobile apps, browser extensions, chat-app bots, plug-in APIs — even voice and AR/VR. New interfaces also mean new feedback channels: easy for users to give in natural language, harder to extract.

Build order, flipped (Shawn Wang, "The Rise of the AI Engineer")

Traditional ML

1 · Gather data → 2 · Train model → 3 · Build product

Product comes last; ML engineers rarely involved in product decisions.

AI engineering

1 · Build product → 2 · Invest in data → 3 · Invest in model

Models are ready-made — ship first, invest only once the product shows promise. Rewards fast iteration.

This pulls AI engineering toward full-stack development: JavaScript tooling is booming (LangChain.js, Transformers.js, OpenAI Node, Vercel AI SDK), and full-stack engineers' edge is turning ideas into demos fast. As Anton Bacaj put it: "AI engineering is just software engineering with AI models thrown in the stack."
